Product Strategy Brief

Money Muscle

A savings-and-chores habit builder for EveryDollar families, prepared as a walkthrough artifact for a Product Manager or Senior Product Manager interview.

Author Phil Albritton Status Working prototype
01

The problem

Two rounds of qualitative discovery point at the same gap. Roughly twenty interviews conducted for the Kids & Money research, plus a separate synthesis of interviews and lobby feedback on the Financial Peace Kids product, both surface the same three complaints: the tools available are too dense to start, they do not flex across age or family size, and parents are left guessing how to reinforce the habit day to day.

Meanwhile, commission for chores is already a normal line item in many EveryDollar budgets. Money is already moving to kids. Nothing turns that transaction into a lesson.

03

Target user

Primary: EveryDollar parents in the Baby Steps and FPU orbit, with kids age six to fourteen who already give an allowance or a chore commission.

Acting user: the kid, during the split itself. The product's core interaction belongs to a six-to-fourteen-year-old, not the parent holding the account.

Deliberately out of scope for this version: teens fifteen and older. Both research sets flag that this age group wants something less babyish than a bucket-splitting game. Building a teen experience without testing that assumption first would be guessing.

04

The loop

A parent gives money, directly or through a paid chore. The kid splits it into Give, Save, and Spend and gets a small celebration for finishing. A streak counter and a named savings goal give the habit a reason to repeat. The chore board gives the parent a reason to open the app between paydays, not only on allowance day.

05

From insight to feature

Each shipped feature traces back to a specific complaint from the research, not a hypothetical one.

Customer signalWhat shippedWhy it matters
"The Parent Guide is too dense to start." Give money and split it in two taps. No setup screen. Removes the single most common activation complaint.
"One kit doesn't fit a family with more than one kid." Separate profile, history, streak, and goal per kid. Matches how families actually use one household budget.
"Parents want a goal tracker, not just a chore chart." Named savings goal with a progress bar and its own achievement screen. Makes delayed gratification visible instead of abstract.
"Consistency is harder to sustain than the initial idea." Streak counter, plus chores that reset on a daily, weekly, or monthly schedule. Removes the manual re-setup that causes these systems to quietly lapse.
"Giving is an underused emotional hook." Optional note and photo attached to what a Give was for. Turns generosity into something visible and worth repeating.
"Commission for chores is already a normal budget line." Full chore board: parent sets a price, kid submits photo proof, parent pays, requests a redo, or dismisses. Converts an existing transaction into a teaching moment instead of a silent transfer.

How success gets measured

The prototype has no instrumentation and no defined success metric today. This is what I would track before calling the feature validated, framed as targets to test rather than results already in hand.

North Star — Weekly Split Rate
≥ 60%

Share of active kid profiles that complete at least one Give / Save / Spend split in a rolling 7-day window.

48-Hour Activation
≥ 70%

Newly added kids who complete a first split within 48 hours of being given money.

≥ 3 days

Median consecutive-day streak length among kids active at day 30.

Chore Response Time
< 24 hrs

≥ 35%

≥ 15%

07

Why this matters to the business

The Financial Peace Kids research already puts a number on the underlying opportunity: a stated goal of growing that product line's revenue 25%, from $109,000 to $136,250 a year. Money Muscle does not compete with that goal, it de-risks it. A digital feature carries almost none of the fulfillment cost of a physical kit, and reaches every EveryDollar household with kids, not only the ones who bought a box.

The stronger case is retention. A family with young kids stays on a budgeting product longer when the product earns a place in the household's routine beyond the parents' own budget. A chore board that pays out through EveryDollar money gives a family a daily reason to open the app that has nothing to do with reconciling a transaction.

08

Where this plugs into EveryDollar

The prototype runs on local device storage, which is the right way to prove the interaction loop and the wrong foundation to ship on. Folding this into EveryDollar's existing account system does three things at once: it fixes the parent-and-kid-on-separate-devices gap that local storage cannot solve, it lets a real transaction on the kids-commission budget line trigger the split automatically instead of the parent re-typing an amount, and it lets a Save decision point at a real EveryDollar Fund instead of a second, disconnected goal tracker.

09

Trust and privacy

This product touches a child's photos and activity, which makes privacy a launch gate, not a polish item. Before any real household uses this: a COPPA-aligned consent flow that a parent, not the child, completes; account creation gated behind the parent's own EveryDollar login rather than a separate child sign-up; no third-party analytics or advertising code touching child data; and a clear, working delete path for a child's photos and history.

None of this exists in the prototype today. It was deliberately deferred so the interaction loop stayed the thing being tested, not a compliance exercise. It is the first thing that has to be built before this leaves prototype status.

10

How I would validate it

Everything here was built against secondary research: two existing rounds of customer discovery, not a new study commissioned for this feature. Before trusting it further, I would run five to eight moderated sessions with current EveryDollar parents who already have a kids or commission line, watching the give-split-chore loop rather than asking about it. Then a four-week opt-in beta with twenty to thirty households, instrumented against the metrics in Section 06, before any wider rollout. That beta is also where I would re-test the teen age-band assumption directly instead of guessing at it.

11

Deliberate tradeoffs

A few decisions here were made on purpose, not by accident.

  • Cut a separate teen experience. Both research sets ask for one, but shipping it without testing the assumption first would be a guess wearing a feature's clothes.
  • Cut a standalone savings-goal system in favor of eventually pointing Save at a real EveryDollar Fund. Two trackers for the same number is worse than one.
  • Accepted local-device storage as a known limitation of the prototype, not a design choice for the eventual product. Phase 2 depends on real account infrastructure.
  • Left instrumentation, consent flow, and go-to-market out of the prototype and into this brief instead. Building them before the core loop was proven would have spent effort on the wrong risk first.
12

Rollout

  • Phase 1
    Prove the loop

    Ship the prototype against existing research: give, split, chore board, goals, streaks. Done.

  • Phase 2
    Plug into EveryDollar

    Shared accounts, transaction-triggered giving, Fund-linked goals, COPPA-aligned consent.

  • Phase 3
    Validate with real families

    Moderated sessions, then a 20-to-30-household beta instrumented against Section 06.

  • Phase 4
    Launch to the highest-intent cohort first

    EveryDollar households that already have a kids or commission budget line, ahead of a general release.
